Practical guide

Downsizing

Moving from a larger house to a smaller one is a logistics problem, a financial problem and, for most households, an emotional one. This guide deals with all three in the order they actually arrive.

Start with the floor plan, not the boxes

The most common downsizing mistake is sorting possessions before knowing what the new house will hold. It produces months of work on the wrong problem and it is demoralising.

Get the floor plan of the new property with dimensions. Measure your existing furniture. Lay it out to scale, room by room, and decide what physically fits. That exercise usually takes an afternoon and it converts an open-ended emotional question into a closed practical one: this room takes one sofa, not two, and here is which sofa.

Ceiling heights matter as much as floor area. A dining table that suits a room with nine-foot ceilings can overwhelm one with eight. Storage matters more than either. Houses built in the last twenty years in warm-weather markets frequently have less enclosed storage than an older northern house with a basement and an attic, and there is usually no basement at all.

A sequence that works

  1. Decide the destination first. Everything downstream depends on the size and storage of the new house.
  2. Separate the categories. Things that go, things that are sold, things that are given to named people, things that are donated, and things that are disposed of. Five destinations, not two.
  3. Start with the easiest room. Not the attic and not the study. A guest bedroom or a linen closet builds momentum without emotional cost.
  4. Handle the documents early. Deeds, titles, policies, wills, tax records, medical records. Digitise what can be digitised, shred what should be shredded, and keep originals of the things that must exist on paper in one place that travels with you.
  5. Get valuations before you decide. Jewellery, art, silver, firearms, musical instruments, collections and some furniture categories can be worth substantially more or substantially less than expected. Value first, decide second.
  6. Give named items to named people while you are present. It is easier now than in an estate later, and it prevents a category of family difficulty.
  7. Then choose the disposal route for what remains, from the options below.
  8. Move last, not first. Every box you do not sort is a box you pay to transport twice.

What each disposal route actually returns

Expectations are usually set by television rather than by the market. The honest picture:

Estate sale. A company runs a two or three day sale in the house and takes a commission on gross proceeds, commonly in the range of a third to a half depending on the market and the contents. Best for a whole-house clearance where volume matters more than maximising any single item. Ask what happens to what does not sell, and get it in the contract.

Auction. Suits genuinely collectible items with an established market. The house takes a seller's commission and often a buyer's premium sits on top for the buyer. Timelines run to months, and reserve policy matters. Good outcomes for the right item, disappointing for ordinary household goods.

Consignment. A shop takes items and splits the proceeds when they sell, typically around half. Slow, selective, and the shop chooses what it will take. Reasonable for good-quality furniture in current styles.

Direct sale. Online marketplaces return the most per item and cost the most in time and hassle. Fine for a small number of valuable, easily described items. A poor use of your remaining months for a houseful of goods.

Donation. Fast, and a receipt may support a deduction if you itemise, subject to substantiation rules and appraisal requirements above certain values. Ask your CPA rather than assuming, and get the paperwork at the time.

Disposal. A container is not a defeat. For a full household there is always a meaningful volume of material with no market, and paying to remove it promptly is usually cheaper than storing it while you look for a buyer who does not exist.

Storage. The route that quietly costs the most. Storage is appropriate as a bridge with a defined end date. It is expensive as a decision you have deferred, and units rented "for a few months" during a move are routinely still rented three years later.

Items that need specialist handling

  • Firearms, which have transport and transfer rules that vary by state
  • Wine collections, which need temperature-controlled transport
  • Pianos and large instruments
  • Art above a certain value, which usually needs its own transit insurance
  • Safes, which frequently need structural access work
  • Paint, fuel, pool chemicals and compressed gas, which movers will not take
  • Plants, which several states restrict at the border
  • Documents with personal data, which should be shredded rather than binned

Timing against a property sale

The sequencing question is the one that causes the most stress, and there are only three real answers.

Sell first, then buy

Financially the cleanest. You know exactly what you have, you carry one property at a time, and you make an offer without a sale contingency.

The cost is a gap. You will need somewhere to live and somewhere to put your possessions, and you may feel pressure to buy quickly. Budget for a rental and a storage period rather than assuming the timing will align.

Buy first, then sell

Practically the easiest. You move once, you can prepare the new house at your own pace, and you are not negotiating under time pressure at the far end.

The cost is carrying two properties, which means either bridge financing or the liquidity to hold both. It also means selling the old house while it is empty, which usually means staging it.

Simultaneous

Attractive on paper and difficult in practice, particularly across state lines where the two transactions run on different customs and different timetables.

It can be done with careful contract drafting, a post-closing occupancy agreement on one side, and a lender who understands what you are attempting. It fails when one side slips, and one side usually slips.

There is no universally right answer. It depends on your liquidity, your tolerance for carrying two properties, and how competitive the market you are buying into currently is. We will lay out the three options against your actual numbers and tell you which risks we think are worth taking. We will not tell you the market will do anything in particular, because we do not know and neither does anyone else.

A twelve-week working plan

For a household with a defined move date, this is roughly how the work distributes. Extend it if you can; almost nobody wishes they had given themselves less time.

  • Weeks 1 to 2. Floor plan and furniture layout for the new property. Book the mover and, if needed, the estate-sale company; both are scheduled weeks ahead in season.
  • Weeks 3 to 4. Documents and valuables. Digitise records. Get valuations on anything you suspect is worth more than a few thousand dollars.
  • Weeks 5 to 7. Room-by-room sorting into the five destinations. Easiest rooms first.
  • Weeks 8 to 9. Distribute named items to family. Complete donations and get receipts.
  • Week 10. Estate sale or consignment collection.
  • Week 11. Clearance of what remains. Deep clean. Address the utilities and address changes at both ends.
  • Week 12. Move. Keep a separately transported box of essentials and originals that does not go on the truck.

The part nobody schedules

Downsizing a long-occupied house is, for most households, a process of deciding what to keep from a life rather than a logistics exercise, and it takes longer than the calendar suggests because some days you will get almost nothing done. That is normal and it should be planned for rather than treated as a failure of discipline.

Two practical suggestions from clients who have done it well. First, photograph things you are not keeping. A photograph of the workbench, the children's height marks on the doorframe, the garden in June, carries most of what you actually want and none of the cubic feet. Second, set a small number of exceptions in advance: a defined number of items that get to come regardless of whether they fit or make sense. Having a sanctioned category for the irrational makes the rational decisions much easier.

If a household is downsizing under time pressure or after a bereavement, say so when you speak to us. The sequence changes, and there are things we would not put on your list at all.
