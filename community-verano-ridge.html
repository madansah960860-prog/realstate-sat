Verano Ridge

142 estate lots from half an acre to 2.4 acres against the McDowell foothills, sold to custom and semi-custom builders. Completed homes currently asking $1,250,000 to $6,800,000.

Arizona assesses primary and secondary residences differently, and a home that is not your primary residence is taxed at a higher assessment ratio. Ask a CPA how that applies to you.

Location and setting

Verano Ridge occupies 214 acres on rising ground at the base of the McDowell range, with the community's northern boundary against a state trust parcel and its eastern boundary against a regional preserve. Elevation runs from 1,780 feet at the gatehouse to 2,140 feet at the highest lot, which produces a genuine temperature difference of two to four degrees between the bottom and top of the community on a summer night.

The site was platted in 2007, stalled through the financial crisis, and restarted in 2013. Roughly 104 of the 142 lots are built out. The remaining lots are held by the developer and by three builders, so you will see construction activity in the community for several more years. Ask which lots are still to be built and where the haul route runs before you choose a homesite.

Desert hillside development carries constraints that flat-land development does not. Cut and fill are limited by ordinance, native plant salvage is required, and lots above a defined slope threshold have a reduced buildable envelope. A 2.4-acre lot does not mean 2.4 acres of usable ground. Get the natural area open space calculation and the disturbance envelope for any lot before you value it.

Property types available

There are no attached homes and no condominiums here. Everything is a detached single-family home on its own lot.

  • Completed custom homes, 4,200 to 9,400 square feet on 0.9 to 2.4 acre lots. Built 2014 onward, mostly single level with some partial lower levels cut into the slope.
  • Semi-custom homes from three approved builders, 3,100 to 5,200 square feet on 0.5 to 1.1 acre lots, chosen from a plan library with elevation and finish options.
  • Unimproved lots, 0.5 to 2.4 acres, sold with a design review process and a build-start requirement that varies by parcel.

Most homes here are single level with zero-step entry at the front door and at least one bathroom with a curbless shower, because that is how the plan library was drawn. Nine homes have a casita separated from the main house by a courtyard.

Fees and design review

The association fee runs $395 to $610 per month depending on lot size and sub-plat. It covers the staffed gatehouse, private road maintenance, the trail easement, common desert landscape management including invasive species control, wash maintenance, and reserves. It does not cover your own landscaping, your private driveway beyond the road easement, or utilities.

The design review committee has real authority here and exercises it. Exterior colour is restricted to a palette drawn from the surrounding rock. Roof material, wall height, driveway paving, exterior lighting and glass reflectivity are all controlled. Outdoor lighting is tightly limited under a dark-sky ordinance that the county and the association both enforce. If you intend to build or substantially remodel, read the design guidelines before you buy the lot, not after.

Amenities

This is a low-amenity community by design. There is no golf course, no clubhouse and no restaurant. What there is: a staffed gatehouse with 24-hour coverage, 3.4 miles of private trail connecting to the regional preserve trail network at two points, a small ramada with shaded seating at the trailhead, and a fenced dog run.

Golf, tennis, dining and fitness are all available at clubs within four to nine miles, on separate memberships that have nothing to do with the association. That separation keeps the association fee low, and it is the main structural difference between this community and Aldermere.

Climate by season

The Sonoran desert has a long comfortable season and a genuinely severe summer. There is no way to describe June through September here that makes it sound mild, and we are not going to try.

Airports and hospitals

Phoenix Sky Harbor International (PHX) is 31 miles southwest, generally forty to sixty minutes. It is a major hub with dense year-round nonstop service nationally and several international routes, which is the single strongest practical argument for this part of the country over more remote warm-weather markets. Scottsdale Airport (SDL) is 9 miles south and handles general aviation. Phoenix-Mesa Gateway (AZA) is 41 miles southeast with a limited low-cost route map.

The nearest hospital with a twenty-four hour emergency department is 7.2 miles southwest, roughly fourteen minutes. A larger medical campus with cardiac, stroke and oncology programmes is 11 miles south. Two urgent-care clinics sit within five miles. Verify current service lines directly with the hospital.

Getting around

Car-dependent, without qualification. Distances from the gatehouse: nearest full grocery 4.1 miles, pharmacy 4.0 miles, hardware store 5.6 miles, nearest restaurant cluster 4.4 miles, nearest hospital 7.2 miles. There is no sidewalk on the connecting road, no bicycle lane on the first two miles of it, and no fixed-route transit within four miles. Valley Metro's nearest park-and-ride is 8.9 miles away.

Inside the gates the private roads are paved and lightly trafficked, and the trail easement is used heavily for walking and running between October and April. Golf carts are not permitted on the connecting public road.

Ride-hailing coverage is generally reliable in season. Pick-up wait times at the community lengthen noticeably in July and August.

What daily life here actually involves

From October to April the weather is the reason people are here: clear, dry, warm days and cool nights, with outdoor living genuinely usable from breakfast to late evening. Trail use is constant. Restaurants and clubs in the surrounding area are busy and take reservations seriously. Traffic on the arterial roads increases materially between January and March.

From June to September the community is quiet and the heat organises the day. Outdoor activity happens before 8am or after 8pm. Pool water reaches temperatures that stop being refreshing without a chiller, which is a real line item here. Air conditioning runs continuously, and a house of this size can carry a summer electricity bill several times its winter bill. Ask sellers for twelve months of utility records, not a summer average.

Monsoon season brings brief violent storms between early July and mid-September: microburst winds, blowing dust that reduces visibility to near zero on the highways, and short intense rainfall that runs through the desert washes fast. Wash crossings on the approach road can become impassable for an hour or two. Homes here are required to keep landscape debris clear for wildfire mitigation, and the association inspects for it.

Wildlife is part of living against a preserve. Coyotes, javelina, bobcats and rattlesnakes are all routinely present. The association's guidance on pet management, refuse storage and outdoor feeding exists for that reason and is enforced.

Owners away for the summer typically arrange a weekly property check, set the thermostat to a maintained maximum rather than switching the system off, shut the water at the main, and have irrigation monitored, because a failed drip line in July can kill mature desert planting in under a fortnight.

Rental rules

Arizona limits how far a homeowner association can restrict rentals, and the declaration here reflects that. Leases must be at least thirty days, tenant information must be registered with the association, and the owner remains responsible for compliance with the design and conduct rules. Short-term vacation rental of under thirty days is prohibited under the declaration. Confirm the current state law position and the current declaration language before relying on this.
