State guide

Florida

The largest seasonal-residence market in the United States, and the one with the widest gap between the marketing and the operating reality. This guide covers climate, taxes, costs, metropolitan areas, airports and storm exposure.

Informational only

This page describes how things generally work in Florida. It is not tax, legal, insurance or financial advice, and it is not a promise of any outcome. Tax and residency results depend on facts specific to you. Speak to a licensed CPA, a tax attorney or a financial adviser before acting, and to a licensed insurance agent about coverage. See our Tax and Legal Disclaimer.

Climate

Florida is not one climate. The panhandle behaves like the lower South, with genuine winter cold snaps. Central Florida is warm and humid with occasional frost inland. South Florida and the Keys are tropical, with no meaningful winter at all. Anyone comparing Naples to Jacksonville is comparing two different propositions.

What the whole peninsula shares is a dry season roughly from November to April and a wet season roughly from May to October. The wet season brings daily afternoon convective storms, high humidity and the hurricane threat. The dry season is why the state has a seasonal population at all.

Two things surprise buyers from cooler states. First, winter in central and southern Florida is genuinely comfortable but not uniformly warm; a January cold front can drop the overnight temperature into the forties in Tampa and the fifties in Naples, and most houses are built and insulated for cooling rather than heating. Second, summer humidity is the constraint rather than temperature. A 92-degree day at 75 percent relative humidity is a different experience from a 105-degree day in Phoenix.

Property tax, in outline

Florida has no state personal income tax. Local government is funded substantially through property tax, which is assessed by the county property appraiser and levied at a millage rate set by the county, the municipality, the school board and various special districts.

Two mechanisms matter enormously to a second-home buyer.

The homestead exemption reduces the taxable value of a property that is your permanent residence, and it is available only on a permanent residence. A second home does not qualify. Claiming it on a property that is not your permanent residence is a serious matter and carries penalties and back taxes.

The assessment cap limits how fast assessed value can rise. For homesteaded property the annual increase is capped tightly; for non-homestead property the cap is looser. Because the cap resets when a property changes hands, a long-held house may be taxed on a value far below its market value, and a buyer inherits the market value rather than the seller's historic assessment. The tax line on a listing is very often the seller's tax, not yours. Ask the county property appraiser to estimate what the bill becomes after a transfer.

Florida also offers portability, which allows a homesteaded owner moving within Florida to carry accumulated assessment savings to a new Florida homestead, subject to limits. This is relevant if you eventually make Florida your permanent residence and then move again within the state.

Cost-of-living context

The absence of a state income tax is real and it is also only one line in a household budget. Against it, the state has above-average property insurance costs in coastal counties, above-average auto insurance in the southern metropolitan areas, and housing costs that rose substantially in the first half of the 2020s. Utilities are moderate but cooling load is high. Groceries and services track close to the national average outside the resort markets and above it inside them.

Whether the overall picture works in your favour depends on your income structure, your assets, where you are currently domiciled and how many days you will actually spend in each state. That calculation is a CPA's job and the answer differs household to household. Anyone who tells you Florida is cheaper without asking about your circumstances is selling you something.

Hurricanes, flooding and insurance, stated honestly

Florida has more hurricane landfalls than any other state. Hurricane season runs from June 1 to November 30, with the statistical peak in mid-September. Every coastal county has evacuation zones, and a great many desirable properties sit inside them. This is not a reason to avoid the state. It is a reason to price the risk properly and to buy a house that is built and insured for it.

What actually causes loss, in rough order: storm surge on the coast, inland flooding from rainfall, wind damage to roofs and openings, and then the long tail of water intrusion and mould afterwards. Surge is the killer and it is a function of elevation and geography rather than construction quality.

Practical implications for a buyer:

  • Get the flood zone determination and, where one exists, the elevation certificate for the specific parcel. Zones change street to street.
  • Understand that a standard homeowner policy excludes flood. Flood is a separate policy, from the National Flood Insurance Program or from a private carrier.
  • Wind coverage may sit in the homeowner policy or may be separated out with its own deductible, typically expressed as a percentage of insured value rather than a flat sum.
  • Prefer impact-rated glazing to shutters where the choice exists, and confirm the roof's age and material. Both affect premium materially.
  • For condominiums, read the milestone structural inspection and the structural integrity reserve study. Florida law tightened both after 2021 and the financial consequences are still working through buildings across the state.
  • Get a bindable insurance quote for the specific address during your inspection period. Do not rely on the seller's premium.

We have advised clients to walk away from Florida properties on the insurance number alone, and we will do it again. It is a legitimate reason not to buy a house.

What we tell clients looking at Florida

Decide the airport first, then the county, then the community, then the house. Buyers who do it in the reverse order fall for a house and then discover it is ninety minutes from the airport their children fly into.

Treat insurance as a purchase criterion rather than a closing formality. Two houses with the same asking price can differ by twenty thousand dollars a year in carrying cost, and the difference is visible in the roof, the glazing, the elevation and the association's finances.

For condominiums, read the building before the unit. The finishes are a weekend of work; a structural remediation programme is a decade of assessments.

And be honest with yourself about the summer. Many seasonal owners never spend a July in Florida and are perfectly happy. Some intend to and discover they do not want to. The house does not care either way, but your budget does, because a house occupied two months a year has a very different cost per night of use than one occupied seven.
