Property taxes and homestead exemptions

The tax figure on a listing is almost never the tax figure you will pay. This guide explains why, how assessment and millage work, and how Florida, Texas and Arizona each treat a property that is not your primary residence.

Read this first

Property tax law is set by state statute and administered county by county, it varies within states, and it changes. Nothing here is a promise of any tax saving or any particular assessment outcome. Verify every figure with the relevant county property appraiser, appraisal district or assessor, and consult a licensed CPA or tax attorney about your circumstances. How a property tax bill is built

Almost every American property tax bill is the product of two things: a value the taxing authority assigns to your property, and a rate applied to that value by each unit of local government with authority to tax it.

The value side has several layers. There is market value, which is what a willing buyer would pay. There is assessed value, which is derived from market value and may be limited by a cap or reduced by an assessment ratio. And there is taxable value, which is assessed value less any exemptions you qualify for. It is taxable value that the rate is applied to.

The rate side is usually the sum of several separate levies: county, municipality, school district, and any special districts for fire, water management, community development or similar. In Florida this is expressed as millage, meaning dollars per thousand of taxable value. In Texas it is expressed as a rate per hundred dollars of value. In Arizona there are separate primary and secondary levies.

Neither side is under your control, but both are public. Every county in the three states we cover publishes parcel-level data, and the appraiser or assessor will usually tell you what a bill would look like after a sale if you ask.

Why the listing's tax figure misleads

Three mechanisms cause the gap.

Caps reset on transfer. Where a state limits annual increases in assessed value for a qualifying owner, a long-held property may be assessed far below market value. When it sells, the cap generally resets to the new market value. A house whose seller pays $8,000 a year can generate a bill of $19,000 for the buyer, with nothing about the house having changed.

Exemptions do not transfer. The seller's homestead or residence-based exemptions belong to the seller, not to the property. If you will not qualify, the bill you inherit excludes them.

Classification can change. In states that assess owner-occupied residential property differently from other residential property, a house that was the seller's primary residence and becomes your second home may move to a less favourable classification.

The correct approach is straightforward: before you make an offer, ask the county to estimate the post-sale bill at your expected purchase price and under the classification that will actually apply to you. Every county in our markets will do this, and it takes a phone call.

Questions for the county before you offer

  1. What is the current assessed and taxable value, and what exemptions are on the parcel now?
  2. What would the assessed value become on a sale at my expected price?
  3. What is the total rate for this parcel, including every special district?
  4. Are there any special assessments, community development district obligations or improvement bonds attached to the parcel?
  5. Do any exemptions I might qualify for have a filing deadline, and when is it?
  6. Is there any pending reassessment or district rate change I should know about?

Florida

Florida's homestead exemption reduces taxable value for a property that is your permanent residence, and it carries with it a tight cap on annual assessment increases. Both are available only on a permanent residence. Non-homestead property has a looser cap, which limits volatility but does not prevent substantial increases over a long hold.

Filing has an annual deadline and is made with the county property appraiser. Florida also permits portability of accumulated assessment savings between Florida homesteads within limits.

Claiming homestead on a property that is not your permanent residence, or claiming a residence-based exemption in Florida while claiming one in another state, is treated seriously and can result in back taxes, interest and penalties. If you are unsure whether you qualify, ask the property appraiser rather than guessing.

Texas

Texas funds local government heavily through property tax and effective rates are among the highest in the country. The homestead exemption reduces taxable value for a principal residence and also caps the annual increase in appraised value for that residence.

For a second home neither applies. The appraisal district values the property at market each year and the bill follows the market. In a rising market this compounds, which is why we recommend modelling the tax line forward over five years rather than looking at the current figure alone.

Texas has an active annual protest process with a firm deadline. An out-of-state owner should either diarise it or appoint an agent, because the opportunity does not roll over.

Arizona

Arizona applies an assessment ratio that depends on classification. Owner-occupied residential property, meaning the owner's primary residence, is assessed at a lower ratio than residential property that is not owner-occupied. A second home generally falls into the higher-ratio class, so the same house can carry a different bill depending on how it is classified.

A rebate mechanism has historically reduced part of the bill for primary residences and does not apply to second homes. The details of these provisions have changed over time.

Arizona limits growth in the value used for the primary levy, which moderates year-to-year movement. Appeals run through the assessor and then a state board, with annual deadlines.

Special districts and the fees that hide inside the tax bill

In master-planned communities the tax bill frequently includes an obligation that has nothing to do with the county. Community development districts in Florida, municipal utility districts in Texas and various improvement districts elsewhere issue bonds to fund infrastructure and then levy an annual assessment on each parcel to service the debt.

These assessments can be substantial, they can run for decades, and they are separate from the homeowner association fee. In some communities the bond can be paid off early at the owner's option, and whether the seller has done so materially affects what the property costs to hold.

Ask specifically: is there a district assessment on this parcel, what is the annual amount, how many years remain, and has any portion been prepaid. A listing that quotes an association fee and a tax figure without mentioning a district obligation is not giving you the full carrying cost.

What we do for buyer clients

As part of representation we pull the parcel record from the county, identify every exemption currently on it, identify every taxing unit and special district that levies against it, and produce an estimate of the post-sale bill at your offer price under the classification that will apply to you.

We put that number alongside the association fee, the insurance quote and the utility history so that you are comparing total carrying cost between properties rather than asking prices. Two houses at the same price frequently differ by twenty or thirty thousand dollars a year once all four lines are on the table.

What we do not do is tell you what your tax position will be, or advise you on residency. That is your CPA's work, and we will hand over everything we have gathered to make it easier.
