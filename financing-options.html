Financing options for a second home

Financing a property you occupy part of the year works differently from financing a primary residence, and financing a condominium works differently again. This guide explains the categories so you can ask a lender better questions.

How lenders classify the property

The single most consequential thing about a second-home loan is which of three boxes the lender puts the property in. The classification affects the down payment, the pricing, the documentation and sometimes whether the loan is available at all.

Primary residence. The property you occupy as your principal home. Generally the most favourable terms and the lowest down payment options.

Second home. A property you occupy for part of the year, typically required to be suitable for year-round occupancy, kept under your exclusive control, and not subject to a rental or management agreement that gives control to someone else. Terms are usually less favourable than for a primary residence and better than for an investment property. Lenders generally expect a meaningful distance from your primary residence, and they will look sceptically at a "second home" fifteen minutes from your existing house.

Investment property. A property held to generate rental income. The highest down payment requirements and pricing of the three, with the offsetting possibility that projected rental income may be counted toward qualifying in some programmes.

Do not describe a property as a second home on an application if you intend to rent it out on a short-term basis. Occupancy misrepresentation on a mortgage application is fraud, and it is a category of problem that does not resolve quietly. If your plan involves rental income, tell the lender at the outset and take the investment-property terms.

Condominiums have a second approval

For a condominium the lender approves the borrower and separately reviews the project. A building can fail project review for reasons entirely outside your control: insufficient reserves, deferred maintenance, unresolved structural findings, an excessive share of units in arrears, an excessive share of units under single ownership, an excessive share of commercial space, or pending litigation.

Since 2021 lender scrutiny of condominium structural condition and reserve funding has tightened considerably, and a number of coastal buildings have found themselves temporarily unfinanceable. That has two consequences for a buyer. First, establish early whether the building is currently eligible under the programme you intend to use. Second, understand that a building that fails project review has a narrower buyer pool, which affects resale.

Ask the association directly for its questionnaire responses and its reserve position. Ask your lender to run project review before you spend money on an inspection.

Loan structures people use

  • Conforming loans, within the limits set for the county, offered by most lenders on standardised terms.
  • Jumbo loans, above those limits, priced and underwritten by individual lenders with more variation between them. Documentation requirements are usually heavier and reserve requirements higher.
  • Portfolio loans, held on the lender's own balance sheet rather than sold, which allows flexibility on properties or borrowers that do not fit standard criteria. Often available through regional banks and private banks.
  • Asset-based or securities-backed lending, where qualification rests on assets under management rather than income. Common for borrowers whose income has become irregular. The risk is that a market decline can trigger a demand for additional collateral.
  • Adjustable-rate mortgages, which can suit a defined holding period but require you to understand the adjustment mechanism, the caps and what happens at the end of the fixed period.
  • Cross-collateralised or bridge arrangements, used to buy before selling an existing home. Convenient and expensive; understand the exit before you enter.

Questions for a lender in the first call

  1. How will you classify this property, and what changes if I ever rent it?
  2. What is the minimum down payment and the reserve requirement for this classification?
  3. For a condominium, will you run project review before I order an inspection?
  4. What documentation do you need if my income is largely from investments or a business?
  5. How long is your process from application to clear-to-close for this property type?
  6. What happens to my rate lock if the closing is delayed by the association or the appraisal?
  7. Do you lend in this state and on this property type at all?

Ask the last question first. Some national lenders decline coastal condominiums or unimproved lots outright.

Paying cash, and the trade-offs

A substantial share of second-home purchases in these markets are cash transactions. Cash removes financing contingencies, shortens the timeline and can matter in a competitive situation. It also concentrates capital in an illiquid asset and forgoes whatever return that capital was earning.

Two mechanisms are worth knowing about. Delayed financing allows a cash buyer to take out a mortgage shortly after closing, subject to programme rules, effectively giving you the negotiating position of a cash buyer and the capital structure of a financed one. A home equity line on an existing property can fund a purchase without disturbing an existing low-rate first mortgage, though it introduces a variable rate.

Whether either is sensible for you is a question about your whole balance sheet, and it is a question for a financial adviser rather than a brokerage.

Costs beyond the loan

Budget for the appraisal, which on an unusual property in a thin market can be slow and can come in below contract. Budget for title work and, in some states, a title policy whose cost is a matter of local custom in terms of who pays. Budget for the condominium estoppel or resale certificate fee, the association transfer fee, and any capital contribution the association levies on a transfer, which in some communities is a multiple of the monthly fee.

And budget for the insurance premium as a real number rather than an estimate, because on the coast the lender will require evidence of wind and flood coverage before closing and the premium is due at closing.

What can delay a second-home closing

  • Condominium project review. The association is a third party with no deadline pressure. Start early.
  • Insurance binding. In coastal counties, carriers can decline or take weeks. Some will not bind during an active named storm, which can stop a closing outright in September.
  • Appraisal in a thin market. Few recent comparable sales means a slower appraisal and more risk of a value shortfall.
  • Association estoppel and transfer paperwork. Statutory timeframes exist in some states but the practical wait can still be significant.
  • Survey and title issues. Encroachments, unpermitted structures and dock permits surface here and can take weeks to resolve.
  • Remote signing logistics. If you are not in the state, arrange notarisation and wire instructions well in advance, and verify wire instructions by voice with a number you already had. Wire fraud in real estate closings is common and the money is rarely recovered.

We build these into the timeline when we write an offer, and we would rather ask for a longer financing period than watch a deal fail at the end of a short one.
