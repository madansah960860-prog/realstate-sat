The Seasonal Living Guide

Owning a house you occupy for part of the year is a different discipline from owning one house. This is the hub for everything that follows from that: closing the property, insuring it, keeping it dry, keeping the mail moving, and understanding what it costs when nobody is in it.

Start here

Most people arrive at a second-home purchase having thought carefully about the house and hardly at all about the eight months of the year they will not be in it. That is the expensive gap. A property that sits closed through a Gulf Coast summer or a Sonoran July is under a different kind of load from a property that is lived in continuously, and the failures are predictable: humidity, water, pests, batteries, and the slow degradation of anything with a rubber seal.

The guides linked from this page are written for a reader making a major financial decision. They are long, they are specific, and where the honest answer is "ask a professional in that state" we say so rather than guessing on your behalf.

None of this is tax, legal, insurance or financial advice. It is a description of how things generally work, written by a brokerage, and every one of these subjects turns on facts specific to you.

The five numbers we ask about first

  1. How many months a year will the house be occupied?
  2. How far do you want to be from an airport with year-round nonstop service to where your family is?
  3. What monthly association fee is genuinely acceptable to you?
  4. What is your insurance tolerance if wind and flood premiums rise again?
  5. Who closes the house if a storm is named while you are 1,400 miles away?

If the fifth question has no answer yet, that is normal, and it is solvable. It is also the one people discover too late.

Closing the house for the off-season

The single most useful habit a seasonal owner develops is a written close-up list, worked through in the same order every departure. What belongs on it varies by climate, but the structure does not.

Water

Shut the supply at the main valve, not just at fixtures. Most catastrophic interior losses in unoccupied houses are supply-line failures: a braided washing-machine hose, an icemaker line, a toilet fill valve. Drain what freezes if the climate freezes. Turn off the water heater or set it to its vacation setting. If you have an irrigation system running while you are away, and in a desert climate you probably must, put it on a monitored controller with a flow sensor so a broken line raises an alarm rather than a bill.

Air and humidity

In a humid climate, do not switch the air conditioning off. Set it to hold a temperature and, if the system supports it, a humidity setpoint in the mid-fifties. Clear and treat the condensate line before you leave, because a blocked condensate line in August is how ceilings come down. Consider a standalone dehumidifier plumbed to a drain in a closed-up coastal house.

Power and connectivity

Leave the internet connection on. Everything you might want to monitor remotely depends on it. Add a water-leak sensor at the water heater, under each sink and at the laundry, and a temperature and humidity sensor in the main living space. These are inexpensive and they are the difference between a two-hundred-dollar problem and a forty-thousand-dollar one.

Pests and food

Empty the refrigerator and prop the doors. Remove every food item including pantry staples and pet food. Clear the outdoor grill. In desert and coastal climates alike, an empty house is an attractive one.

Storm readiness

If the property is in a hurricane-exposed location, the house should be left in a state where a local contact can close it quickly. Shutters accessible and tested, outdoor furniture with a designated storage place, a written plan the home-watch service holds, and current photographs of every room for insurance purposes stored somewhere that is not the house.

The costs people underestimate

Purchase price gets the attention. Carrying cost decides how the ownership feels in year three.

  • Insurance. In coastal Florida and coastal Texas this is frequently the largest single line after the mortgage, and it has moved sharply upward across the last several years. Get a bindable quote before you commit, not an estimate.
  • Association fees. Read the reserve study, not just the current fee. An underfunded association is a future assessment.
  • Home watch. Weekly interior checks typically run a few hundred dollars a month. It is the cheapest insurance you will buy.
  • Utilities you pay while away. Air conditioning maintained at a humidity setpoint, pool circulation, irrigation, and the base connection charges on everything.
  • Pool and landscape. Continuous, whether or not you are there, and in a desert climate a pool chiller is a genuine summer expense.
  • Travel. Two people, twice a year, plus the trips you had not planned when a contractor needs you present.
  • Duplicated household. You will buy a second set of nearly everything. Budget for it once rather than being surprised by it repeatedly.

Mail, vehicles, healthcare and voting

Mail forwarding through the postal service is temporary and imperfect. A permanent solution is usually a mail service at the address you treat as your base, with everything important redirected there and forwarded on request. Do not let an insurer or a lender learn your occupancy pattern from returned mail.

Vehicles left standing need a battery tender, fuel stabiliser in some circumstances, and tyres inflated above normal to reduce flat spotting. Confirm with your insurer how a vehicle stored in a second state affects the policy, because garaging address matters to auto insurers in ways that surprise people.

Healthcare continuity is worth arranging deliberately: a physician in each location, records that move between them, prescriptions filled by a national chain that can transfer, and a clear understanding of how your insurance handles out-of-network care in the state where you spend the winter. Original Medicare travels well across states; Medicare Advantage plans have networks that frequently do not. Check this with your plan before you rely on it, not after.

You vote in one place. Which place you register in is one of several facts a state will look at if it ever examines where you are domiciled, which is why the residency and domicile guide asks you to treat these choices as a set rather than one at a time.

A first-season timeline

What the first twelve months usually look like once you have closed.

  1. Week one

    Utilities, access and the document set

    Transfer every utility into your name and confirm the billing address. Register with the association and collect gate credentials, amenity fobs and the rules in writing. Rekey or recode every exterior lock. Photograph every room and store the photographs off site. File the survey, the elevation certificate if there is one, the insurance policies and the association documents in one place you can reach from anywhere.

  2. Month one

    Trades, monitoring and the local contact list

    Meet the air conditioning contractor, the plumber, the electrician, the pool service and the landscaper before you need any of them urgently. Install leak and temperature sensors. Choose a home-watch service and agree in writing what a visit includes and what happens in a storm. Give the association a local emergency contact.

  3. Months two to four

    Live in it before you change it

    Resist the urge to renovate immediately. A season in the house tells you which rooms get the light you expected, where the noise comes from, and which of the seller's decisions were actually correct. Architectural review in most of these communities takes weeks, so plan the timeline backwards from when the work must finish.

  4. Departure

    The close-up list, written down

    Work the list in the same order every time and leave a copy with the home-watch service. Confirm insurance is current and that the policy has no vacancy or occupancy condition you are about to breach, because some policies do.

  5. Off-season

    Reports, not silence

    Ask for a short written report with photographs after each home-watch visit. Read them. A rising indoor humidity reading three weeks in a row is a problem announcing itself early.
