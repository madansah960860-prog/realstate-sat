The first season in a seasonal home: a checklist that assumes nothing

Closing is the beginning of the work, not the end of it. This is the sequence, including the items most owners only discover in year two.

Buying a house you will occupy for part of the year introduces a category of task that owning one house does not. The property has to run without you in it, and it has to be brought back to life twice a year. Almost everything that goes wrong in the first two years of seasonal ownership traces back to something on this list not being done, usually because nobody said it out loud.

This is our working checklist, in order. It is written for a warm-weather property occupied roughly November to April, but the structure applies anywhere.

Week one: control of the property

Rekey or recode every exterior lock, including the garage keypad, the pool gate and any lockbox left behind. You have no idea how many keys exist. Contractors, cleaners, previous tenants and family members of the previous owner may all have one.

Transfer every utility into your name with the correct billing address, and set them all to paperless with online access. Electricity, water, sewer if separately billed, gas or propane, waste collection, internet and any irrigation meter. Confirm the propane tank ownership, because a leased tank has a contract that transfers with the house.

Register with the association and collect gate credentials, amenity fobs, vehicle decals, the rules in writing and the management company's after-hours number. Give them a local emergency contact; most associations require one and it is the number they will call when something happens.

Photograph everything. Every room, every elevation, the roof if you can see it safely, the mechanical equipment with serial numbers visible, and the contents. Store the photographs somewhere that is not the house. This is your insurance documentation and it takes an hour.

File the document set in one place you can reach from anywhere: survey, elevation certificate, permits, warranties, association documents, insurance policies, inspection reports and the closing package.

Month one: the people

Meet your trades before you need them urgently. At minimum: an air conditioning contractor, a plumber, an electrician, a pool service if there is a pool, a landscaper, and a general handyman. Get them to the house for something small so they have seen it, know where the shut-offs are, and have you in their system. A contractor who has been to your house once will answer the phone in July. One who has never heard of you will not.

Then choose a home-watch service and agree in writing what a visit includes, how often it happens, what the written report contains, and specifically what happens when a storm is named. Not all home-watch contracts include storm preparation, and discovering that from 1,400 miles away is a bad way to find out.

Finally, arrange the healthcare side: a physician locally, records transferred or accessible, prescriptions with a chain that can fill in both states, and a clear understanding of how your health insurance handles care in this state. Original Medicare travels between states straightforwardly; many Medicare Advantage plans have networks that do not. Check with your plan before you need it.

Month one: the monitoring

This is the highest-return spend in the entire list and it costs a few hundred dollars.

  • Water leak sensors at the water heater, under every sink, behind the washing machine and at the air handler drain pan.
  • A temperature and humidity sensor in the main living space, ideally two, with alerting.
  • A smart water shut-off valve on the main if the plumbing configuration allows it. This is the single device most likely to prevent a catastrophic loss.
  • A connected thermostat so you can see whether the air conditioning is actually running rather than trusting that it is.
  • Keep the internet connection active year-round. Everything above depends on it. Cancelling the internet to save forty dollars a month disables your entire monitoring system.

Months two to four: live in it before changing it

Resist renovating immediately. A season in the house teaches you things a tour cannot: which rooms get the afternoon light, where the road noise comes from at 6am, whether the primary suite is on the right side of the house, and which of the previous owner's decisions were actually correct. A surprising number of planned renovations get cancelled after one season, and a few get much better.

If you do intend to make changes, start the architectural review process early. In most managed communities approval takes weeks and the committee may meet monthly. Plan the timeline backwards from when the work must finish, not forwards from when you decided.

Use the season to build the property's operating file: which breaker controls what, where the irrigation valves are, which way the pool valves turn, the filter sizes, the paint codes and the model numbers. Write it down. In year three you will not remember, and neither will anyone else.

Departure: the close-up list

Write it once, work it in the same order every year, and leave a copy with the home-watch service.

  • Water off at the main. Drain what should be drained. Water heater off or on its vacation setting.
  • Air conditioning left running on a temperature and humidity setpoint. Condensate line cleared and treated. Filters changed.
  • Refrigerator emptied and doors propped, or left running if someone will check it.
  • All food removed, including pantry staples and pet food.
  • Outdoor furniture, umbrellas and anything loose stored or secured.
  • Shutters accessible and tested, or impact glass confirmed on every opening.
  • Vehicles on a battery tender, tyres inflated, and the insurer notified if the garaging arrangement changes.
  • Mail redirected properly rather than left to accumulate.
  • Internet, monitoring and alerting confirmed working before you leave the driveway.
  • Storm plan confirmed in writing with the home-watch service.
  • Insurance confirmed current, and checked for any occupancy or vacancy condition. Some policies have them.

The off-season: read the reports

Ask for a short written report with photographs after every home-watch visit, and actually read them. Indoor humidity climbing three weeks in a row is a problem announcing itself while it is still cheap. A photograph of a stain on a ceiling in week six is worth considerably more than discovering it in November.

Diarise the annual items so they do not all arrive at once: insurance renewal, association fee changes, the property tax bill and its deadline, air conditioning service, termite bond renewal, and, in Texas, the appraisal protest window, which does not roll over if you miss it.

The things people discover in year two

A short list of what our clients most often say they wish they had done in year one.

  • Installed the automatic water shut-off valve rather than deciding it was excessive.
  • Bought a second set of basic tools, a ladder and a vacuum instead of transporting them.
  • Asked the seller for twelve months of utility bills before closing rather than budgeting from an average.
  • Set up the association's payment on a schedule that does not depend on being in the state.
  • Kept a written contractor list with the work each one has done, rather than searching old email.
  • Taken the roof photographs at closing, giving a clear before-and-after record for any future storm claim.
  • Introduced themselves to two neighbours who are there year-round. This is the least technical item on the list and the one most often described as the most useful.

None of this is difficult. It is simply a category of work that nobody warns you about, because the transaction ends at closing and the ownership begins there.
