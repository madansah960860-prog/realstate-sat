Residency and domicile

If you own homes in two states, two states may each have a view about where you live. This guide explains the framework so you can have a better conversation with a professional. It is not that conversation.

Read this first

We are not accountants, tax attorneys or financial advisers, and nothing here is a recommendation to take or refrain from taking any action. State residency and domicile rules are fact-specific, they vary substantially by state, and they change. We make no promise of any tax saving or any particular outcome. Before you act, consult a licensed CPA, a tax attorney or a financial adviser who can review your full circumstances. See our Tax and Legal Disclaimer.

Two different words

Residence is generally about presence. A state may treat you as a resident for tax purposes because you were physically there for a defined number of days, or because you maintain a permanent place of abode there and met a day threshold. Several states use a statutory test of this kind.

Domicile is about intent and permanence. Your domicile is, broadly, the one place you treat as your true, fixed and permanent home, and the place you intend to return to whenever you are away. You can have several residences. You have one domicile at a time. Changing it requires both leaving the old one and establishing the new one with the intent to remain.

The two concepts interact awkwardly, and that is the source of most of the difficulty. You can cease to be domiciled in a state and still be taxed by it as a statutory resident if you keep a home there and spend enough days there. That combination catches people who move their domicile south and keep the family house up north.

Why "183 days" is not the whole test

The day count is the part everyone has heard of, and it is genuinely important where a state applies a statutory residency test. But it is one factor among many, and it does not by itself settle domicile.

Several practical complications follow. Days are counted differently in different states; some count any part of a day present, with narrow exceptions for transit. Records matter, and the burden of proving where you were usually falls on you rather than on the state. And a state auditor examining domicile will look well beyond the calendar.

What states actually examine

The factors below appear repeatedly in state guidance and in reported residency cases. No single one decides the question, and the weight given to each varies.

  • Where you spend your time, supported by contemporaneous records rather than reconstruction.
  • The size, value and use of each home you own or rent, and which one you treat as the principal one.
  • Where your immediate family lives.
  • Where you are registered to vote and where you actually vote.
  • Where your vehicles are registered and where your driving licence was issued.
  • The address on your federal and state tax returns.
  • Where your primary bank accounts, safe deposit box and financial advisers are.
  • Where your physicians, dentists and other professional relationships are.
  • Where your items of near-irreplaceable sentimental value are kept.
  • Business connections, board seats and active professional licences.
  • Club, place of worship and community memberships.
  • Mailing address and where mail is actually delivered.
  • Any formal declaration of domicile, homestead filing or similar statement.

The pattern that gets people into difficulty is a partial move: the declaration filed in the new state, the day count achieved, and everything else, family, doctors, advisers, memberships and the larger house, left behind. Auditors look at the whole picture.

Departing state audits

Some states audit departing residents actively, particularly where the income at stake is significant. Expect any examination to reach back several years and to request calendars, credit card records, mobile phone records, EZ-Pass and toll data, utility usage at each property, and travel bookings. This is another reason to keep records at the time rather than assembling them later.

None of this is a reason to avoid owning property in two states. It is a reason to plan the change deliberately with a professional, to do it thoroughly rather than partially, and to document it as you go.

Questions worth taking to a CPA

  1. Given my income structure, does changing domicile actually change my overall tax position, and by how much?
  2. Does my current state apply a statutory residency test, and would keeping the northern house trigger it?
  3. How does my state treat trust income, deferred compensation, stock compensation and retirement distributions sourced to it?
  4. What records should I be keeping from the first day, and in what form?
  5. Are there filings with deadlines I need to make in the year of the change?
  6. What happens to my estate plan, and does it need to be redrafted for the new state?

If a professional answers all of these confidently in a first meeting without seeing your returns, get a second opinion.

What a brokerage can and cannot do

We can tell you what documents a county requires, how homestead filing works procedurally in the states where we are licensed, what our other clients have found practical, and introduce you to accountants and attorneys.

We cannot tell you whether to change your domicile, calculate your tax position, or promise you a saving. Any brokerage that does either is exceeding what its licence permits.

A sequence people commonly follow

This is a description of steps others have taken, in the order they usually take them. It is not a recommendation, and the right sequence for you depends on facts we do not know.

  1. Before anything

    Get the analysis done

    A CPA models your position under both states before you change anything. For some households the difference is substantial. For others, particularly where income is sourced to the old state regardless of where you live, it is smaller than expected and may not justify the disruption.

  2. Establish

    Build the new connections

    Driving licence, vehicle registration, voter registration, primary banking, physicians, insurance policies, professional advisers, and the address used on federal filings. Do these deliberately and keep the confirmations.

  3. File

    Make the formal filings

    Where the new state offers a declaration of domicile or a homestead filing, make it within the applicable window. Deadlines are real and missing one usually costs a year.

  4. Sever

    Reduce the old connections

    This is the step most often left half done. Consider what happens to the former primary residence, memberships, and any exemption previously claimed in the old state. Continuing to claim a residence-based benefit in a state you have left is a serious problem, not a technicality.

  5. Document

    Keep contemporaneous records

    A day-by-day location log, kept as you go. Boarding passes, tolls, card transactions and utility usage patterns all corroborate it. Reconstruction three years later is far weaker evidence than a record kept at the time.

  6. Review

    Revisit the estate plan

    Wills, trusts, powers of attorney and healthcare directives are governed by state law and may need to be redrafted rather than merely re-signed. Property held in two states can create a second probate. Ask an estate attorney licensed in the new state.

Common misunderstandings

"I bought a house there, so I live there."

Owning property is one factor. It does not by itself establish domicile, and it does not prevent another state from treating you as a resident.

"I spent 184 days, so I am done."

The day count matters where a statutory test applies, but domicile turns on intent and permanence evidenced by a whole pattern of behaviour. Meeting the day count while leaving your entire life in the other state is a weak position.

"A mail forwarding address is enough."

It is not, and a mail-drop address with no corresponding physical presence tends to attract attention rather than deflect it.

"My accountant handled it years ago, so it is settled."

Rules change, and so do circumstances. A change in where family lives, where a business operates or how much time you actually spend somewhere can change the analysis.

Where this touches the property decision

Occasionally the tax analysis changes what you should buy, not only where you should file. A household intending to change domicile may want the southern house to be plainly the principal home in size and character rather than plainly the smaller secondary one. A household with no intention of changing domicile has no such constraint and may sensibly buy something more compact.

Similarly, the homestead exemption available in some states applies only to a permanent residence, which means the carrying cost of a property can differ depending on your residency position. That belongs in your model before you choose a price range, not afterwards.

Tell us where you are in this process when we first speak. It changes what we shortlist, and it is one of the few areas where the tax question genuinely feeds back into the property question.
