Arizona

A long, dry, comfortable season from October to April, a genuinely severe summer, no coastal storm exposure, and a water supply picture that any buyer with a twenty-year horizon should understand before committing.

Informational only

Climate

Arizona is not uniformly hot desert. Elevation governs everything. Phoenix sits at roughly 1,100 feet and is genuinely severe in summer. Tucson at 2,400 feet runs several degrees cooler and greener. Prescott at 5,300 feet and Flagstaff at 7,000 feet have four seasons, snow in winter and a summer that is pleasant rather than punishing.

For a seasonal buyer the dominant pattern in the Sonoran desert is a long comfortable season from mid-October to late April, with clear skies, very low humidity and a large day-to-night temperature swing. That swing is the underrated feature: a 78-degree January day drops to the mid-forties overnight, and outdoor living is genuinely usable across most of the day.

Summer is the counterweight and there is no version of it that is mild. From June to September daytime temperatures in the Phoenix area routinely exceed 105°F and overnight lows in the city stay above 85°F for extended stretches. The urban heat island effect is real and measurable, and communities on higher ground at the metropolitan edge do run cooler overnight.

The monsoon arrives in early July and runs to mid-September. It brings the humidity the rest of the year lacks, along with brief violent storms: microburst winds that take down mature trees, blowing dust that reduces highway visibility to nothing, and short intense rainfall that moves fast through the desert washes. Wash crossings can become impassable for an hour or two. Roofs and pool equipment take the damage.

Property tax, in outline

Arizona property tax is low by national standards and the mechanism differs from Florida and Texas in a way that matters specifically to second-home buyers.

The county assessor establishes a value, and then applies an assessment ratio that depends on how the property is classified. Owner-occupied residential property, meaning the owner's primary residence, is assessed at a lower ratio than residential property that is not owner-occupied. A second home generally falls into the higher-ratio class. The practical effect is that the same house can carry a meaningfully different tax bill depending on classification.

There is also a rebate mechanism that has historically applied to primary residences and reduced the school district portion of the bill. Second homes do not receive it. The rules here have changed over time and can change again, so treat any figure you read, including ours, as a starting point to verify with the county assessor and a CPA.

Arizona also limits how fast the assessed value used for the primary levy can grow, which moderates year-to-year volatility relative to Texas.

Cost-of-living context

Arizona has a state personal income tax at a flat rate that applies to residents, which distinguishes it from Florida and Texas and is a genuine factor in any domicile comparison. Against that, property taxes are low, homeowner insurance is moderate because there is no hurricane or coastal flood exposure, and housing cost outside the highest-value Scottsdale and Paradise Valley submarkets is reasonable relative to coastal states.

The line item that surprises people is electricity. A large house in the low desert with a pool can run a summer electricity bill several times its winter bill. Solar generation is common and effective here, and whether an array is owned outright or leased materially affects both the monthly cost and the resale. Ask for twelve months of utility records rather than an average, and ask specifically about the solar arrangement.

Water is billed modestly at the household level. The longer-term question is not the bill.

Heat, monsoon, wildfire and water, stated honestly

Extreme heat is the primary hazard in the low desert, and it is a genuine one. Extended stretches above 110°F occur most summers. Air conditioning failure in July is an emergency rather than an inconvenience, which is why a second air conditioning system, or at least a serviceable one with a recent inspection, is worth more here than the equivalent equipment elsewhere.

Monsoon storms cause most of the property damage: wind loss to roof tiles and pool cages, debris impact, and localised flash flooding in and around washes. If a property sits near a wash, ask for the drainage report and look at where water goes in a hundred-year event, not a normal one.

Wildfire matters at the desert and forest edges. Communities against preserve land carry defensible-space obligations, and insurers increasingly rate on proximity to fuel. In the higher-elevation forested markets, fire risk is a primary insurance and safety consideration rather than a secondary one.

Water is the long-horizon question. Arizona's supply comes from the Colorado River, in-state rivers, reclaimed water and groundwater, and the Colorado River system has been in a sustained shortage condition. Municipal providers in the established metropolitan area operate under a designation requiring a demonstrated hundred-year assured water supply, which is a meaningful protection. The pressure has fallen most heavily on new development on the fringe that relies on groundwater without such a designation, and the state has restricted some new subdivision approvals on that basis.

For a buyer, this translates into practical questions rather than alarm: who is the water provider, does the community sit within a designated assured water supply area, is the property on municipal water or a private well, and if a well, what is the depth, the yield and the shared-use agreement. Ask these before you buy a lot in particular.

Major metropolitan areas and airport access

Phoenix Sky Harbor is one of the strongest practical arguments for Arizona over more remote warm-weather markets: a large hub with dense year-round service and a short drive from most of the desirable submarkets.

What we tell clients looking at Arizona

Ask for twelve months of utility records on every house you shortlist. In this state that single document tells you more about the true cost of ownership than any other, and it exposes a failing air conditioning system, an oversized pool, or an under-performing solar array.

Understand the tax classification before you model the carrying cost. A second home is generally not assessed at the primary-residence ratio, and the difference is not trivial. Ask a CPA rather than assuming.

On hillside lots, buy the disturbance envelope rather than the acreage. Slope ordinances, natural area open space requirements and design review determine what you can actually do with the ground, and a large lot with a small buildable envelope is worth less than its size suggests.

And confirm the water provider and the assured water supply position in writing, particularly for lots and for newer communities at the metropolitan fringe. This is the question that will matter most over a twenty-year hold and the one that gets asked least at the point of purchase.
