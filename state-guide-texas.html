State guide

Texas

A state large enough to contain four distinct climates, no personal income tax, comparatively high local property taxes, and a coastal strip with its own insurance framework. This guide covers all of it.

Informational only

This page describes how things generally work in Texas. It is not tax, legal, insurance or financial advice, and it is not a promise of any outcome. Tax and residency results depend on facts specific to you. Speak to a licensed CPA, a tax attorney or a financial adviser before acting, and to a licensed insurance agent about coverage. Climate

Texas spans humid subtropical on the Gulf Coast, semi-arid on the southern plains, arid in the far west, and something closer to the lower Midwest in the panhandle. For a seasonal buyer the realistic candidates are the Gulf Coast, the Hill Country, and the lower Rio Grande Valley, and they behave very differently.

The Gulf Coast from Galveston Bay south is humid, mild in winter and heavy in summer, with genuine hurricane exposure and a fog season on the bays in late winter. Winter here is cooler than Florida: hard freezes are uncommon but they happen, and the February 2021 freeze caused widespread pipe failures across a region whose houses were not built for it.

The Hill Country west of Austin and north of San Antonio is drier, several degrees cooler at night through the summer thanks to elevation, and prone to flash flooding in the river valleys. No coastal surge exposure, but hail is a real and expensive risk.

The lower Rio Grande Valley has the mildest winter in the state and a long, hot summer, with a seasonal population pattern much like Florida's.

Two statewide features to plan around. First, hail: Texas records more damaging hail than any other state, and it drives homeowner premiums as much as wind does. Roof age and material matter to your insurer here as much as they do on the Florida coast. Second, the electrical grid: after February 2021, standby generation moved from a luxury to a common specification in higher-value Texas houses, and it is worth asking whether a property has it.

Property tax, in outline

Texas has no state personal income tax, and it funds local government substantially through property tax. Effective rates are among the highest in the country. A property is appraised each year by the county appraisal district at market value, and multiple taxing units, typically the county, the city, the school district and one or more special districts, each levy a rate against that value.

The homestead exemption reduces the taxable value of a principal residence and also caps the annual increase in appraised value for that residence. Both benefits attach to a principal residence. A second home does not qualify for either.

That second point deserves emphasis, because it is where Texas differs most sharply from what buyers expect. Without the homestead cap, the appraised value of a second home can rise with the market each year, and the tax bill rises with it. In a rising market that is a real and compounding carrying cost. Model it forward rather than looking only at this year's figure.

Texas allows property owners to protest their appraisal annually, and the process is used widely. A second-home owner who is out of state during the protest window should understand the timetable or appoint an agent.

Cost-of-living context

Housing costs across most of Texas remain moderate relative to coastal states, though Austin and parts of the Hill Country moved sharply upward in the first half of the 2020s. Utilities are moderate, with a high summer cooling load. Groceries and services sit close to or slightly below the national average outside the largest metropolitan cores.

The trade-off is explicit rather than hidden: no income tax, high property tax, and coastal insurance that in the counties nearest the Gulf is expensive. Whether the whole picture favours you depends on your income structure, your asset base, where you are currently domiciled and the value of the property you buy. That is a CPA's calculation and it varies household to household.

Hurricanes, freezes, hail and insurance, stated honestly

The upper Texas coast has a serious hurricane history. Storm surge on the shallow bays is the primary risk to property, and it is a function of elevation and geography. Coastal counties have evacuation zones and a great many attractive waterfront properties sit inside them.

Insurance on this coast is typically split across three policies. A homeowner policy that in many coastal cases excludes windstorm. A separate windstorm and hail policy, which along the coast is frequently written through the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association when private capacity is unavailable. And a flood policy, which is always separate. Windstorm coverage generally requires certification that the structure meets the applicable building standard, so for any rebuilt or renovated coastal house the certificate is a document you ask for by name.

Inland, the dominant risk is different. Hail drives claims and premiums across a wide band of the state, and roof age is a primary rating factor. Some carriers now apply a separate percentage deductible for wind and hail, or depreciate roof payouts by age. Read the declarations page rather than the summary.

Freeze risk is statewide and is the one that catches second-home owners specifically. A vacant house with unprotected exterior plumbing during a hard freeze is a burst-pipe claim waiting to happen, and in February 2021 those claims arrived in the tens of thousands across the state simultaneously. For a seasonal property, freeze protection is part of the close-up routine, not an afterthought.

Practical implications for a buyer:

  • Get the flood zone determination and the elevation certificate for the specific structure.
  • Get a bindable windstorm quote, and the windstorm certification, during the option period.
  • Confirm roof age, material and how the policy treats roof depreciation.
  • On waterfront property, have the bulkhead inspected by a marine contractor and confirm dock permits.
  • Ask whether the property has standby generation and how it is fuelled.

What we tell clients looking at Texas

Model the property tax forward, not backward. A second home in Texas carries no homestead cap, so run the numbers on what the bill looks like in five years under a range of appraisal growth assumptions, not on what the seller paid last year.

On the coast, do the insurance work before the inspection work. Windstorm, flood and the bulkhead are the three numbers that decide whether a waterfront property is a sensible purchase, and all three can be established during the option period.

Inland, ask about the roof and the generator in the first conversation. Those two items tell you a great deal about how the property has been maintained and what your carrying cost will look like.

And take the freeze seriously. It is the risk that Florida and Arizona buyers do not think to plan for, and it is the one that most reliably damages an empty Texas house.
