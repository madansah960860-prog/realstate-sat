Selling your current home

Most of our seller clients are selling one property in order to buy another. That changes the sequencing, the pricing conversation and the risk we are managing on your behalf.

The listing agreement and our fee

Our fee is agreed in writing in the listing agreement and paid from your proceeds at closing. It is negotiable, there is no standard rate, and the agreement states the amount, the term, the scope, what happens if you withdraw, and any protection period after expiry.

Separately, you decide whether to offer compensation to a brokerage representing a buyer, and if so how much. That decision is yours, it is negotiable, and it is not required. We will explain how the choice affects the pool of buyers likely to see and pursue your property, and then you decide. We do not make that decision for you and we do not present it as fixed.

Where we are also representing you on the purchase of a second property, that does not change our seller fee and there is no bundled discount that quietly costs you more elsewhere. If we ever represent both sides of the same transaction, the disclosure and consent rules of that state apply and we will explain exactly what we can and cannot do for each side before anyone signs anything.

Pricing from evidence

The listing price is the single decision that most affects the outcome, and it is the one most often made emotionally.

We price from closed comparable sales, adjusted for the differences that a buyer will actually notice: location within the neighbourhood, condition, systems age, layout, outdoor space and view. We show you the comparables, the adjustments and the reasoning, and we show you the sales we excluded and why. If you disagree with an adjustment, argue with it. That conversation improves the number.

We will give you a recommended range and our honest view of where in it to start. What we will not do is agree to a price we think is wrong in order to win the listing and then spend three months asking you to reduce it. That practice is common and it costs sellers real money, because a property that sits accumulates days on market and buyers read that as a signal regardless of the reason.

We also will not forecast the market. We can tell you what has sold, at what price, in what time, and how current inventory compares to the same month last year. We cannot tell you what prices will do next and neither can anyone else.

Questions to ask any listing agent

  1. What is your fee, and what does it cover?
  2. What is the term of the agreement and how do I cancel?
  3. Which comparable sales support your price, and which did you exclude?
  4. What is your marketing plan specifically, item by item?
  5. Who conducts showings, you or someone else?
  6. How and how often will you report to me?
  7. What is your recommendation on compensation to a buyer's brokerage, and why?
  8. Have you sold in this specific community before?

How a listing runs

  1. Step one

    Walkthrough and written opinion of value

    We walk the property, then send a written opinion of value with the comparables, the adjustments and a recommended range. No charge and no obligation to list with us.

  2. Step two

    Preparation plan with costs

    A specific list of what to do before listing, each item with an estimated cost and our view of whether it returns more than it costs. Many do not. We will tell you which repairs matter to a buyer's inspector and which cosmetic work is a waste of your money.

  3. Step three

    Pre-listing diligence

    We assemble the disclosure package before the property goes live: survey, permits, warranties, association documents, insurance history, roof and systems documentation, and any elevation certificate. Problems found now are negotiating items. Problems found by a buyer's inspector in week six are price reductions.

  4. Step four

    Preparation and photography

    Cleaning, repairs, decluttering and, where it earns its cost, staging. Photography scheduled for the time of day that actually suits the property's orientation. Floor plan drawn to scale. Video where the property warrants it.

  5. Step five

    Launch

    MLS listing with complete and accurate data, syndication to the major portals, our own database, direct outreach to agents who have sold in the community, and print or digital advertising where the property justifies it. Every marketing item is listed in the agreement so you can see what you are getting.

  6. Step six

    Showings and feedback

    Scheduled through a system you control, with a written report to you every week that includes showing count, portal activity, agent feedback verbatim and our reading of it. If the feedback says the same thing three times, we bring you a recommendation rather than waiting for you to ask.

  7. Step seven

    Offers and negotiation

    We present every offer with an analysis: price, but also the financing type, the deposit, the contingency structure, the closing date and the buyer's likely ability to perform. The highest number is not always the best offer, and on a coastal property an offer contingent on an insurance quote carries a real risk that a cash offer does not.

  8. Step eight

    Under contract to closing

    Inspection response, appraisal management, association paperwork, title, and the walkthrough. Where an appraisal comes in below contract we prepare a written challenge with the comparables rather than assuming the number is final.

Selling a house you no longer live in

An empty house shows differently. Every scuff is visible, rooms read smaller without furniture to give scale, and sound carries. Staging an empty property, even lightly, usually earns its cost, and we will tell you when it does not.

Empty houses also need active maintenance while listed. Air conditioning maintained at a humidity setpoint, water running through the traps periodically so they do not dry out and admit sewer gas, landscaping kept up, mail collected, and the whole thing checked weekly. Nothing costs a sale faster than a buyer walking into a stale house with a dry trap and a dead lawn.

If you have already moved, tell us early so we can arrange access, key control and property care rather than discovering the arrangement does not exist on the first showing.

The sequencing problem

Selling and buying at the same time across two states is where most of the real risk sits. The three options and their trade-offs are set out in the downsizing guide, and we work through them against your actual liquidity rather than in the abstract.

Where you are buying through us in one of our markets and selling elsewhere, we will refer the sale to a brokerage licensed in that market and disclose our referral fee in writing before you speak to them. That fee comes out of their commission and does not increase what you pay.

Disclosure obligations

Seller disclosure requirements vary by state and are taken seriously in all of them. Broadly, you must disclose known material defects that are not readily observable. Failing to disclose something you knew about is the most common route to litigation after a sale, and it is entirely avoidable.

Where a property has flooded, has had a roof claim, has had structural repair, has an unpermitted addition, has a known drainage problem, has active or historic termite treatment, or sits under a pending association assessment, disclose it. In our experience buyers price a disclosed and repaired problem far more mildly than they price a discovered one, and a discovered one usually ends the negotiation.

We will also tell you plainly what we cannot say in marketing. Fair housing law prohibits describing a property or a neighbourhood in terms that indicate a preference or limitation based on a protected class. That rules out a range of phrases that sellers sometimes ask for, including descriptions of who a home would suit, characterisations of the neighbourhood's population, and references to schools framed as a quality judgement. We describe the property and the amenities. Fair Housing Notice.
