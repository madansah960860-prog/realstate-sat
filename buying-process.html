The buying process

From the first conversation to the keys, with the compensation question answered up front rather than at the end.

How commission works, and who pays it

Real estate commission is negotiable. It is not set by law, it is not set by any association, and there is no standard rate. Anyone who tells you otherwise is wrong.

When we represent you as a buyer, we agree our fee with you in writing before we tour a property together. That agreement states the amount or percentage, what it covers, how long it runs and how either of us can end it. Once that is agreed, the question of who actually pays it is separately negotiable, and it usually resolves in one of these ways:

  • The seller offers compensation to the buyer's brokerage, which is applied against our agreed fee.
  • The listing brokerage offers compensation to the buyer's brokerage, applied the same way.
  • You pay us directly at closing.
  • You ask the seller to contribute toward your closing costs as part of the negotiation, and that contribution is applied to our fee.
  • Some combination of the above.

If an offer of compensation from a seller or listing brokerage is less than what you agreed to pay us, the difference is yours to pay. If it is more, we do not keep the excess above the agreed fee. That is stated in the agreement, and you should expect to see it stated in any buyer representation agreement you sign anywhere.

When we represent a seller, our fee is agreed in the listing agreement and paid from the seller's proceeds at closing.

We are a licensed brokerage and commission on representation is our main source of revenue. Our other revenue lines, referral fees from partner brokerages in markets where we are not licensed, fee-based concierge services, and paid community placements labelled as advertising, are all described on the licensing and brokerage disclosures page and summarised on the home page.

Before you sign any representation agreement

  1. What exactly is the fee, and is it a percentage or a flat amount?
  2. How long does the agreement run, and how do I end it early?
  3. Is it exclusive, and what happens if I find a property myself?
  4. Does it cover one market or several?
  5. What happens if the seller offers less than the agreed fee?
  6. Is there a protection period after the agreement ends, and what triggers it?
  7. Who at the brokerage will actually be doing the work?

Ask these of us and of every other brokerage you speak to. Good answers should be immediate.

The eleven steps

Timelines below are typical for our markets. A cash purchase can be faster; a coastal condominium can be considerably slower.

  1. Step one

    Consultation

    Forty-five minutes by phone or video, no charge and no obligation. We ask what you are trying to solve, how many months a year the property will be occupied, what airport access you need, what carrying cost is acceptable and what your timeline is. We tell you honestly whether we are the right brokerage. If the market you want is one where we are not licensed, we say so and offer a referral instead.

  2. Step two

    Representation agreement

    Fee, duration, scope and termination in writing. We will not tour properties with you before this exists, because working without it is unclear for both of us.

  3. Step three

    Financing conversation

    Before touring, speak to a lender about classification, programme and timeline, or confirm your cash position and how funds will be available. We introduce lenders and receive no fee from them. Financing options.

  4. Step four

    Written shortlist

    We search the relevant MLS, plus pocket inventory and off-market opportunities, and return a written shortlist. Each entry carries the asking price, the association fee, the estimated post-transfer tax figure, the insurance range and the reason it is on the list. Properties we think are wrong for you appear with the reason we ruled them out.

  5. Step five

    Touring

    Usually a concentrated two or three day trip rather than repeated visits. We plan the routing around the airport and the times of day that show each property honestly. Where you cannot travel, we tour on video and record it.

  6. Step six

    Community and parcel diligence

    Before an offer, not after. Association budget, reserve study, minutes, insurance certificate, rules and any litigation disclosure. County parcel record, current exemptions, every taxing district and an estimate of the post-sale tax bill. Flood determination and, where relevant, the elevation certificate. For condominiums, the milestone inspection and structural integrity reserve study.

  7. Step seven

    Insurance quote

    On the coast we get a bindable quote for the specific address before the inspection period ends. This has stopped more of our clients' purchases than any inspection finding, and it should.

  8. Step eight

    Offer and negotiation

    We prepare the offer with the contingencies the property actually needs, which for a seasonal property usually means a longer inspection period than a local buyer would take, plus association document review, insurance and, for condominiums, lender project review. We present the analysis behind the number rather than a number alone.

  9. Step nine

    Inspection period

    General inspection, plus whatever the property specifically requires: wind mitigation, four-point, roof, pool, seawall or bulkhead by a marine contractor, septic, well, mould, or a structural engineer. We attend, we read the reports, and we negotiate on the findings that matter rather than every item on the list.

  10. Step ten

    To closing

    Title, survey, association estoppel and transfer paperwork, lender conditions, and the final walkthrough. If you cannot attend, we arrange remote signing. We verify wire instructions by voice using a number we already hold, every time, without exception, because wire fraud in real estate closings is common and the money is rarely recovered.

  11. Step eleven

    After closing

    Utilities, association registration, locks, trades and the first-season list. Included in representation at a basic level; available as a managed fee-based concierge service if you want it handled.

What is different about buying a seasonal property

You are buying a carrying cost, not just a house. Association fee, taxes after transfer, insurance, utilities while empty, pool and landscape, and home watch. Two houses at the same price frequently differ by thirty thousand dollars a year across those lines, and the difference is knowable before you offer.

The inspection period should be longer. You are coordinating specialists remotely and reading association documents that may run to several hundred pages. Local buyers routinely take ten days. We ask for more.

Insurance is a purchase criterion. On the coast, get the bindable quote early. It is the number most likely to change your decision.

Seasonality affects the market itself. In our markets inventory and buyer activity both concentrate between November and April. Buying in August means fewer choices and less competition. Neither is inherently better and we will not tell you the market is about to do anything, because we do not know.

What we will not do

  • Tell you property values will rise. We do not know, nobody does, and any brokerage that promises appreciation is making a claim it cannot support.
  • Create urgency that is not real. If a property genuinely has competing interest we will tell you and show you why. If it does not, we will tell you that too.
  • Describe a community in terms of who lives there. Under the Fair Housing Act we describe the property and the amenities. If you ask us who lives somewhere, we will decline and explain why. Fair Housing Notice.
  • Give tax, legal or insurance advice. We will hand our research to your CPA and your attorney.
  • Accept a referral fee from a lender, inspector, insurance agent, attorney or accountant.
  • Push a paid-placement community. Advertising is labelled as advertising and it does not change what we recommend.
