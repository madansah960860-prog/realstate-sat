Coastal insurance and elevation: what actually drives the premium

On the Gulf coast, insurance is frequently the difference between two houses at the same asking price. Almost all of the variables are knowable before you make an offer.

We have watched clients tour two houses on the same street, both around the same price, both apparently comparable, and then receive insurance quotes that differed by more than twenty thousand dollars a year. Nothing about that outcome was random. Every input was written on a document that could have been requested at the start.

This piece sets out what those inputs are, in roughly the order of how much they move the number. It is general and informational; only a licensed insurance agent can quote your specific risk, and only a bindable quote for a specific address means anything at all.

First, understand that it is not one policy

On the coast you will usually carry more than one policy, and buyers who think in terms of "homeowner insurance" as a single product miss most of the picture.

A homeowner policy covers the structure and contents against a defined set of perils. In many coastal areas it excludes windstorm, and it always excludes flood.

A windstorm and hail policy covers what the homeowner policy carves out. In coastal Texas this is frequently written through the state's windstorm association when private capacity is unavailable; in Florida the structure varies by carrier and by county.

A flood policy, from the federal programme or a private carrier, covers rising water. It is always separate, and for a condominium the association's flood coverage on the building does not extend to the inside of your unit.

For a condominium there is a fourth consideration: loss assessment coverage on your unit-owner policy, which responds if the association's own loss exceeds its policy limits and the shortfall is assessed to owners.

Flood zone and elevation: the largest single variable

Flood premium is driven principally by how high the lowest occupied floor sits relative to the base flood elevation for the parcel, and by the flood zone the parcel sits in.

The document that establishes this is an elevation certificate, prepared by a licensed surveyor. It records the lowest floor elevation, the lowest adjacent grade, the elevation of machinery and equipment, and the flood zone and base flood elevation from the current flood insurance rate map. If a house has one, ask for it. If it does not, and the property is in a mapped flood zone, consider having one prepared during your inspection period. The cost is a few hundred dollars and the information can change your decision.

The practical effect of elevation is large and non-linear. A house whose living level sits several feet above base flood elevation is a fundamentally different risk from one sitting at or below it, and the premium reflects that. This is why so much modern coastal construction puts parking and storage at ground level and the living space above: it is not an architectural preference, it is a response to the way the risk is priced.

Two cautions. Flood maps are revised, and a revision can move a parcel into or out of a mapped zone. And a lender's flood determination is not the same document as an elevation certificate; the first tells you whether insurance is required, the second helps establish what it costs.

Wind mitigation features: the second largest

In Florida, a wind mitigation inspection documents specific construction features that reduce wind loss, and carriers apply credits for them. The features that matter most:

  • Roof covering and whether it meets the current building code, along with its installation date.
  • Roof deck attachment, meaning how the sheathing is fastened to the trusses.
  • Roof-to-wall connection, with clips, single wraps and double wraps each earning progressively more credit than toe-nailing.
  • Roof geometry, where a hip roof performs better than a gable and is credited accordingly.
  • Opening protection, meaning impact-rated glazing or approved shutters on every opening, including garage doors and skylights. Partial protection frequently earns nothing, because the credit is generally all-or-nothing across the whole building envelope.
  • Secondary water resistance beneath the roof covering.

The all-or-nothing point on opening protection is the one that surprises people. A house with impact glass everywhere except one bathroom window and the garage door may receive no opening protection credit at all. Closing that gap can be one of the highest-return repairs available on a coastal house, and it is worth pricing during the inspection period rather than after closing.

Roof age and material

Roof age is a primary underwriting factor across the Gulf coast, and increasingly a binary one. Many carriers will not write a policy on a roof beyond a certain age, and some that will write it apply actual cash value settlement rather than replacement cost, which means depreciation is deducted from any claim payment.

Ask for the roof's installation date, the permit, and the material. A concrete tile or metal roof generally carries a longer expected life than asphalt shingle in this climate. A roof replaced after a storm claim may also have documentation showing it was rebuilt to a later code standard, which helps.

Deductible structure

Coastal wind deductibles are frequently a percentage of the insured value rather than a flat dollar amount. On a house insured for one and a half million dollars, a five percent wind deductible is seventy-five thousand dollars. Two policies can show similar premiums and expose you very differently.

Read the declarations page, find the deductible for each peril, and confirm whether it is per occurrence or annual. Then decide whether you can comfortably fund the deductible, because a deductible you cannot pay is a coverage gap in practice.

Claims history, on the address rather than on you

Insurers look at prior claims associated with the property, not only with the buyer. A property with multiple water claims may be difficult to insure regardless of who owns it. Ask the seller for a loss history report on the address, and ask your agent to run one. A history of repaired claims is not necessarily disqualifying, but discovering it after closing is a poor sequence of events.

For condominiums, the building is the risk

For a condominium your own premium is a fraction of the picture. The master policy sits inside the association fee, and its cost is driven by the building's age, construction, elevation, glazing, roof and claims history. A large increase at renewal arrives as a fee increase for every owner at once.

Read the master policy declarations page, the wind deductible, and the association's reserve position. A building with a large percentage wind deductible and thin reserves is one storm away from an assessment, and that risk belongs in what you are willing to pay for a unit in it.

The practical sequence

  1. Before touring, ask your agent for a rough premium range in the specific county and price band, so you are shopping with a realistic number in your head.
  2. On any property you are serious about, request the elevation certificate, the wind mitigation report, the roof permit and date, and the loss history on the address.
  3. Within the first few days of the inspection period, get a bindable quote for the specific address, not an estimate and not the seller's premium.
  4. Price the gaps. If the opening protection is incomplete or the roof is near the end of its accepted age, find out what closing that gap costs and negotiate on it.
  5. Decide with the number in front of you. It is entirely reasonable to walk away from a house on the insurance figure, and we have advised clients to do exactly that.

None of this makes coastal property a bad purchase. It makes it a purchase with a knowable carrying cost, which is a different thing entirely from an unpredictable one. The buyers who have difficulty are almost always the ones who treated insurance as a closing formality rather than as one of the three or four numbers that decide whether the house works.
