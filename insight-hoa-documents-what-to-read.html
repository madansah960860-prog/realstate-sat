Association documents: what to read, in what order, and what you are looking for

Almost every buyer in a managed community receives the documents and reads the rules. The rules are the least important part.

When you buy into a homeowner association or a condominium, you receive a package of documents that in our markets typically runs between two hundred and eight hundred pages. Most buyers open it, find the section on architectural rules, discover they cannot paint the front door the colour they wanted, feel mildly annoyed, and close the file. Then, two years later, a special assessment arrives and they are surprised.

The rules govern what you may do. The financial documents govern what it will cost you. They are not equally important, and the order in which people read them is almost exactly backwards.

What follows is the order we read them in for buyer clients, and what we are actually looking for in each.

1. The budget, and the two numbers behind the fee

Start with the current operating budget. The monthly fee is an output, not an input, and the budget shows you the two things that produced it: what the association spends to operate, and what it sets aside for the future.

Look first at the largest expense lines. In a coastal condominium the insurance line is frequently the single largest item and it is the most volatile, because the master policy renews annually into a market the association does not control. In a golf or gated community the largest lines are usually landscape, security and road maintenance, all of which are more stable.

Then look at the reserve contribution as a share of the total. An association contributing a small share of its budget to reserves is running a low fee today and accumulating an obligation for later. That obligation does not disappear because it is unfunded. It arrives as a special assessment, and it arrives to whoever owns the unit at the time, which may be you.

Finally, compare this year's budget to the last two. A fee that has risen twelve percent a year for three years is telling you something. So is a fee that has not risen at all in five years while every input cost in the country has.

2. The reserve study

A reserve study is an engineering and financial document that inventories the components the association must eventually replace, estimates the remaining life and replacement cost of each, and models whether current contributions will fund them.

Read three things. The percent funded figure, which compares the reserve balance to the theoretically ideal balance. The components with short remaining life, because a roof with three years left and a reserve balance that will not cover it is a special assessment with a date on it. And the funding plan, which will tell you what fee increases the study assumes and whether the board has actually adopted them.

That last point catches people. A reserve study can model a fully funded outcome on the assumption of a seven percent annual fee increase, and the board can then decline to raise the fee. The study looks reassuring and the plan is not being followed. Compare the study's assumed fee to the actual fee.

In Florida, condominium associations are now required to conduct structural integrity reserve studies covering defined structural components, and the ability to waive funding for those components has been curtailed. If you are looking at a Florida condominium, that document and the association's response to it are the most consequential pages in the entire package.

3. The milestone or structural inspection, where one exists

For buildings of three storeys or more in Florida, a milestone structural inspection is required at defined intervals. The report identifies structural conditions and, where necessary, required remediation.

Ask for the report and, separately, for the association's remediation status. A building with findings and a funded remediation plan is in a reasonable position. A building with findings, no plan and no reserve balance is a different proposition entirely, and it may also be difficult to finance, which narrows your eventual buyer pool as well as your own options.

Do not accept a summary. Ask for the report.

4. Five years of minutes

Board and membership meeting minutes are the least polished and most informative document in the package. Budgets show what the association decided. Minutes show what it argued about.

Read for recurring items. A drainage problem discussed at six consecutive meetings is a drainage problem that has not been solved. Read for litigation, threatened or actual. Read for insurance discussions, particularly any mention of a coverage reduction, a higher deductible or a change of carrier. Read for the phrase "deferred" attached to any maintenance item. And read for turnover: a board that has replaced its management company three times in four years is telling you something about how the association functions.

Minutes are also where you find out about the things that never make it into a disclosure, such as a persistent noise issue, a dispute with a neighbouring landowner, or an amenity that has been closed longer than anyone admits in the marketing.

5. The declaration and the rules

Now read the governing documents. Not for the paint colours, but for the provisions that materially affect value and use.

  • Rental provisions. Minimum lease term, maximum number of leases per year, approval process, and whether the association can impose a cap on the number of rented units. This affects both your own flexibility and your eventual resale market.
  • Maintenance boundaries. Precisely where association responsibility ends and yours begins. In condominiums this can turn on the finished surface of a wall, and it decides who pays for a great many repairs.
  • Assessment authority. How much the board can levy without a member vote, and what majority a larger assessment requires.
  • Amendment provisions. How easily the rules you are relying on can be changed by a future board.
  • Transfer costs. Application fees, transfer fees, and any capital contribution due on a sale, which in some communities is a multiple of the monthly fee and is payable by the buyer.
  • Insurance obligations. What the master policy covers, what you must carry, and any required loss assessment limit.
  • Enforcement. Fines, liens and the association's remedies. Also, in practice, whether the rules are enforced at all, which the minutes will tell you.

6. Insurance certificates

Get the master policy declarations page rather than a summary certificate. Look at the limits, the wind deductible, whether flood is carried on the building, and whether the deductible is expressed as a percentage of insured value. A five percent wind deductible on a building insured for sixty million dollars is a three million dollar deductible, and if a storm produces a loss inside that number, the owners fund it through an assessment.

This is why loss assessment coverage on your own unit-owner policy is not a formality. Ask your insurer for a limit that reflects the size of the building rather than accepting a default.

The questions that follow

By the end of this you should be able to answer: what does the fee actually buy, is the association funding its future obligations or deferring them, is there a known assessment coming, can I use the property the way I intend, and what will I pay on the day I close that is not the purchase price.

If the answer to any of those is unclear, ask the management company in writing and keep the reply. A written answer from the association is worth considerably more than a verbal assurance from anyone, including a broker.

We read these documents for buyer clients as part of representation and produce a written summary with the items we think matter and the questions we think you should ask. We are not lawyers and we do not give legal opinions on the documents. Where something needs a legal reading, we say so and recommend a real estate attorney licensed in that state.
